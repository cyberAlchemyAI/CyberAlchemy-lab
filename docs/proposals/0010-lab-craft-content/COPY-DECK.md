### CMP-01 — Opening and first comparison

**Public copy**

Breadcrumb: **Practical guides / Marketing / Campaign comparison**

# The dashboard is smiling. Should you be?

Campaign A brought in twice as many leads as Campaign B for the same spend. That sounds promising. Before the team changes its budget, let's look at what it wants those leads to mean.

**Editorial / placement:** insert the shared example-standing block EX-01 here.

**Public copy**

## First, put the numbers on the table.

For the selected reporting period, each campaign spent US$1,200. The supplied rows contain 120 leads for A and 60 for B.

| Selected-period observation | Campaign A | Campaign B |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Spend | US$1,200 | US$1,200 |
| Recorded leads | 120 | 60 |
| Cost per recorded lead | US$10 | US$20 |

The arithmetic is straightforward: US$1,200 divided by 120 is US$10. Divide the same spend by 60 and it becomes US$20.

To examine this comparison, keep the dated source rows, reporting window, and calculation method alongside it. A later reader needs to know which observations we used.

### CMP-02 — Criterion and qualified result

**Public copy**

## “Qualified” is doing some work here.

The team also wants to know which leads meet its selected qualification criterion. In this example, that criterion has been accepted for this reporting purpose.

**Q1 · selected qualification criterion**  
A lead has a valid business email and has requested a demonstration.  
Scope: this campaign-reporting comparison. The full criterion record identifies its exact version, owner, and acceptance decision.

Using Q1, the recorded assessments give A 12 qualified leads and B 15.

| Q1 comparison | Campaign A | Campaign B |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Qualified leads | 12 | 15 |
| Cost per qualified lead | US$100 | US$80 |

US$1,200 divided by 12 is US$100; divided by 15, it is US$80. B looks better on this measure.

Both calculations can be correct. They answer different questions. Neither one establishes which campaign caused more revenue, or gives the team permission to spend more money.

### CMP-03 — Inspectable report

**Public copy**

## Give the conclusion somewhere to stand.

**R1 · completed comparison under Q1**  
The report points to the selected Q1 version, the reporting period, its source rows, and the assessment method. It records the 12 and 15 qualified leads and the US$100 and US$80 comparison.

The report's record contract says what must be present. Its sources let us examine the observations and transformations. Its current state tells us which steps have happened and what may follow.

At this point, we can ask three separate questions:

- **Authority:** which meaning applies here, and who is responsible for accepting it?
- **Provenance:** where did these numbers come from, and how did we produce this version of the report?
- **Lifecycle:** where does this report stand, and what changes does its contract permit?

The same questions apply to Q1 and the decision accepting it, with different answers for each subject.

### EVO-01 — Challenge and successor

**Public copy**

## Even the rules need a way to grow.

Later, the team finds that some leads requesting demonstrations never fit its intended customer segment. Someone asks whether Q1 is still the right criterion for this report.

That observation gives the team a reason to investigate. It leaves room for other explanations too: an inaccurate segment assessment, a change in the offer, or a mismatch in the data.

**Q2 · proposed successor**  
Keep Q1's two conditions and require a recorded assessment that the organization fits the selected target segment.

Consider lead L42. It has a valid business email and requested a demonstration, so it qualifies under Q1. It has no recorded target-segment assessment. We cannot yet classify it as qualified under Q2.

A Q2 proposal identifies the changed meaning, its scope, the evidence behind it, and the work that would be affected. The responsible owner examines it through the applicable decision route. Acceptance and adoption for later reporting remain identifiable steps.

A changed criterion might also require a record-contract change. That contract has its own review; the criterion's new name does not decide it.

### EVO-02 — Preserving the past

**Public copy**

## The old report keeps its history.

R1 still points to Q1. Its counts remain 12 and 15 under that criterion. Accepting a successor does not silently change what that earlier report meant.

**R2 · proposed later assessment**  
Q2 is a candidate. The inputs and proposed assessment are identified; counts are still pending.

If Q2 later completes its required acceptance and adoption, a completed R2 can record a new assessment under that exact version. It needs observed counts of its own. We have not supplied them here.

The team could also re-examine the earlier period under Q2. That would be another identifiable assessment, preserving the difference between the original conclusion and a later reading of the same period.

## And then we find out whether it helped.

A new criterion can be carefully accepted and still turn out to be unhelpful. Later application and outcome evidence let the team assess that question.

This is how the example reaches beyond a dashboard. The organization can examine a meaning, develop a successor, and change how later judgments are made while preserving an account of why.

**Explore the environment underneath** → BRAIN  
**See a different kind of work** → REP

Read them together. The request was accepted. An update was promised. No matching payment appears in this export.

## A useful reply can keep a question open.

**Draft reply · awaiting review**

> Your refund request was accepted, and our previous reply promised an update. We still need to confirm whether the payment has been issued, so we can't give you a confirmed payment date yet.

**Basis for the reviewer:** the accepted request, previous correspondence, selected policy revision, export, and its period.  
**Unresolved:** whether a payment exists outside the examined records.  
**Proposed next work:** ask the responsible finance role to investigate payment status.

The draft acknowledges what happened without creating a new unsupported date. Its sources and open question remain available to the person reviewing it.

## Leave the next person a useful starting point.

Record the decision about the reply and the open investigation. Preparing text, assigning work, and sending a message have different effects and need their applicable permissions.

If the experience suggests a lesson about checking payment status before promising a date, describe where that lesson applies and what improvement it predicts. Its later use and outcome still need examination.

### TEAM-08 — Research and strategy

**Public copy**

# A confident answer still owes you its sources.

Three summaries recommend the same direction. Open their citations and you discover that two repeat the first. The agreement is worth understanding before it is counted as support.

**Editorial / placement:** insert the shared example-standing block EX-01 here.

**Public copy**

## Start with a question that can be examined.

For this illustration, ask whether a selected workflow change is supported by the supplied observations. Identify the decision the research will inform, the observation period, and the criteria for considering an alternative.

## Trace the claims before collecting more summaries.

Record what each source actually observes, the conditions it covers, and which claims are interpretation. Link the two derivative summaries back to their shared origin. Three texts can still represent one underlying observation.

**Example brief**  
**Question:** does the selected evidence support a trial of the proposed workflow?  
**Support:** one observation under stated conditions.  
**Alternative explanation:** the observed change may depend on a different team or workload.
