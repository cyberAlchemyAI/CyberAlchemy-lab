# Novelty, classics and lasting engagement

**Date:** 11 October 2026. **Status:** researched synthesis and prepared investigations. **Scope confirmed by the human:** literary classics and academic evidence, connected to new game-mechanic generation and how long a new player keeps playing. This is a targeted review, not an exhaustive or systematic review. No human reading study, gameplay test, simulation or agent dialogue was run in this extension.

## 1. Answer and new direction

Novelty can contribute to engagement, but the evidence does not supply a rule converting novelty into hours played, nor establish that the most novel works last longest. A survey of 390 online game players associated novelty with concentration, satisfaction and commitment; it did not track how long those players subsequently remained. [Kosa and Uysal, LS06](https://academic.oup.com/iwc/article-abstract/38/3/342/7629773).

The research direction worth testing is **renewable discovery**: a familiar work or rule system can support further changes in interpretation, skill or purpose. This is our working synthesis, not a new established psychological construct. Ask what someone can discover on encounter two that they could not understand, notice or perform on encounter one. Compare that with simply supplying more unfamiliar material.

Calvino's account of classics proposes renewed discovery through rereading and changed readers. Shklovsky's defamiliarization describes making familiar things perceptually strange. These are useful theoretical starting points, not evidence that either process causes historical survival. [Calvino, LS01](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1986/10/09/why-read-the-classics/), [Shklovsky, LS02](https://www.paradise.caltech.edu/ist4/lectures/Viktor_Sklovski_Art_as_Technique.pdf).

For the original hammer problem, the resulting question is: **After the hammer becomes familiar, what can it still teach us about the problem?** A proposed game or analysis method earns this claim by producing a consequential new relation or operation, with evidence beyond a renamed familiar action.

## 2. Five outcomes that must remain distinct

| Outcome | Unit and timescale | Evidence to collect | Confusion to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Initial attraction | A person, a reveal or first session | Trial choice, initial interest, premise comprehension | Attention does not establish continued use. |
| Continued engagement | The same person across sessions | Voluntary returns, enjoyment, progress, completion and reasons for stopping | Intention is not an observed return; more hours can mean slower progress. |
| Rereading or replay | The same person revisiting material | Changed interpretation, perceived depth, new tactic, improved skill | Recognition and repetition alone do not demonstrate discovery. |
| Cultural endurance | A work transmitted to successive cohorts | Availability, editions, translations, teaching, reception and voluntary readership | A centuries-old book can be new to a reader who encounters it once. |
| Delayed value | A person after an experience | Recall, reflection, recommendation and transfer | A finished short experience can retain value without continued playing. |

This table is an operational proposal. A work can score highly on one outcome and poorly on another. Separate the survival of the work from the persistence of one person's activity.

Novelty also needs a reference frame: new to the world; new to a genre audience; new to this individual; or newly meaningful within a familiar system. Only the first supports a historical originality claim, and that requires much wider precedent research. A new player can encounter old mechanics as new; an experienced player can recognize their conventional structure despite an unusual premise.

## 3. What literary research contributes

### 3.1 Appreciation can develop after the ending is known

Dixon and colleagues distinguish properties of a text from their effects on a population of readers. Their first experiment, with 45 undergraduate readers, found greater improvement in combined appreciation ratings on a second reading for Borges's *Emma Zunz* than for the comparison story. In a second experiment, removing narratorial ambiguity affected frequent and infrequent readers differently. The authors investigated particular texts and reader groups, through prompted rereading. They did not establish that all classics improve on rereading or that this causes voluntary return over years. [Dixon et al., LS03](https://sites.ualberta.ca/~dmiall/LiteraryReading/Readings/Dixon%20Bortolussi%20Twilley%20and%20Leung.pdf).

This supplies a concrete research method: name the supposedly generative feature, alter it, preserve a comparison, and measure what changes between encounters. It also warns against treating an analyst's elegant interpretation as something every reader actually experiences.

### 3.2 Unusual form can redirect attention

Miall and Kuiken's four studies associated foregrounded story segments with longer reading time, greater strikingness and affect. This is evidence about responses during reading. Longer reading does not by itself mean more enjoyment, future rereading or historical endurance. The proposed transfer is to test whether indirection directs attention toward a consequential relation, rather than simply making comprehension harder. [Miall and Kuiken, LS04](https://sites.ualberta.ca/~dmiall/reading/publish.htm).

### 3.3 Surprise has conditional value

Leavitt and Christenfeld's three experiments report circumstances in which spoilers benefited pleasure through fluency, with qualifications involving story difficulty and spoiler simplicity. Johnson and Rosenbaum's experiment with 412 participants instead favored unspoiled stories on fun, suspense and other enjoyment dimensions. The findings defeat a universal claim that either surprise or foreknowledge always improves an experience. Different materials and measures require comparison; the abstracts alone do not settle every cause of the disagreement. [Leavitt and Christenfeld, LS08](https://benjamins.com/catalog/ssol.3.1.09lea), [Johnson and Rosenbaum, LS09](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/spoiler-alert-consequences-of-narrative-spoilers-for-dimensions-o/).

Knowing a fictional ending differs from knowing a boss's attack sequence. A player may understand the sequence while still having difficulty executing the response. Test narrative surprise, uncertainty about a rule and uncertainty about one's performance separately.

### 3.4 Classics are selected and transmitted

Barré, Poibeau and Camps operationalize the contemporary reception of French literature, with substantial attention to schools. They find textual associations with their canonicity criteria and explicitly discuss the limits of finite definitions and causal interpretation. Their classification is not a novelty score or a longitudinal proof of why a work survived centuries. [Barré et al., LS10](https://culturalanalytics.org/article/id/1309/).

The research implication is to include available but less-canonized works, institutional exposure and reader familiarity. Selecting only surviving classics and then searching for their shared virtues builds survival into the sample. Textual richness, historical circumstances and transmission can coexist; their contributions require separate evidence.

## 4. What game and motivation research contributes

### 4.1 Novelty needs a comprehensible opening

Silvia's four experiments support an appraisal account connecting interest with novelty-complexity and perceived ability to understand. These concern immediate interest, not a universal difficulty optimum or months of game retention. For our candidate, an unfamiliar relation should have clues a player can use to understand and act on it. [Silvia, LS05](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15755222/).

### 4.2 Freshness is one contributor among several

Ryan, Rigby and Przybylski's four studies relate need satisfaction to enjoyment and future-play preference. Study 1 also measured an immediate choice to continue the target game rather than an alternative, associated with competence. Future intention and immediate continuation are distinct outcomes. This research supplies reasons to examine competence, autonomy and relatedness alongside novelty; it does not estimate novelty's causal effect on retention. [Ryan et al., LS07](https://www.rochester.edu/warner/lida/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/02bfe513dd59366750000000.pdf).

Park and colleagues examine logs for 51,104 players in an online multiplayer setting. Achievement and social features differ in predictive importance across player levels. This shows why a single explanation for every phase of play is inadequate. Observational prediction does not identify a novelty intervention, and social findings from multiplayer cannot be imported as requirements for this solo game. [Park et al., LS11](https://arxiv.org/abs/1702.08005).

### 4.3 Duration requires an explicit definition

Viljanen and colleagues distinguish total playtime, session duration, progression and active time, and describe survival analysis for incomplete observations. They also note the difficulty of knowing that a player has permanently quit: return remains possible. Thus a fixed inactivity definition is an operational event, not proof of permanent abandonment. [Viljanen et al., LS12](https://arxiv.org/abs/1701.02359).

For this game, record first-session departure, stage progression, completed runs, voluntary later returns and satisfaction separately. Do not label a satisfied player who finishes all three stages as a failed retention case. No available evidence yields an expected number of hours for the proposed game.

## 5. Hype, endurance and the Goat Simulator connection

The earlier [Goat Simulator case](/home/vlad_/projects/CyberAlchemy-lab/docs/proposals/hammer-bias-strategies/novelty/rhetoric-game-design/2026-10-11/GOAT-SIMULATOR-CASE.md) supports investigating premise, expectations and retellable experience. It supplies no measured novelty-to-retention relationship. Launch interest, player enjoyment, ongoing play and cultural recognition need different observations.

Salganik, Dodds and Watts randomized social-information exposure in an artificial music market involving 14,341 participants. Social influence increased inequality and unpredictability of success. This supports separating private response from socially amplified popularity; its outcome is music choice/downloads, not century-scale endurance. [Salganik et al., LS13](https://www.princeton.edu/~mjs3/salganik_dodds_watts06_full.pdf).

Berger and Le Mens relate adoption speed to abandonment using French and US name histories, alongside evidence from expecting parents. Adoption velocity differs from novelty. This is a cross-domain reason to avoid equating rapid uptake with persistence, not a prediction that surprising games or literary works necessarily fade quickly. [Berger and Le Mens, LS14](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2688844/).

## 6. Ingredients to investigate, without a universal recipe

| Candidate ingredient | Proposed operation in analysis or design | Observation that would challenge its contribution |
|---|---|---|
| A reference frame | Write what the participant already knows and expects. | The proposed novelty is already familiar to the intended audience. |
| A recoverable relationship | Leave evidence that supports a later reinterpretation. | Later explanations are invented retrospectively and unsupported by the work. |
| Ability to understand | Supply clues and feedback while preserving a task to solve. | Players report surprise but cannot explain or use the rule. |
| Purpose and stakes | Make discovery change a meaningful decision or interpretation. | Fresh language leaves every action and consequence identical. |
| Progress in capability | Keep opportunities for better judgment or execution. | Continued time reflects repetition without perceived or demonstrated progress. |
| Stable commitments | Preserve enough learned rules for prior work to remain useful. | Each new stage invalidates planning through unexplained exceptions. |
| An attainable stopping point | Let completion deliver a valued resolution. | More sessions arise only because the endpoint was stretched. |
| Transmission and access | Examine how new cohorts find and can use the work. | Apparent endurance is inferred solely from prestige labels. |

These are research variables assembled for this project. They are neither individually necessary in every genre nor jointly sufficient for creativity, canonization or retention. Familiar comfort, skilled repetition, a single powerful encounter and socially sustained tradition are possible counterexamples to novelty as the sole explanation.

## 7. From principles to new mechanic proposals

The [four worked constructions](/home/vlad_/projects/CyberAlchemy-lab/docs/proposals/hammer-bias-strategies/novelty/literature-longevity/2026-10-11/WORKED-EXAMPLES.md) use three inspected classic texts to generate candidate rule relations:

1. **The Witness Map:** revisit known combat under a different, evidence-bearing view of causation.
2. **The Creature's Errand:** a maze redirects an enemy's purposeful delivery; the useful route depends on what arrival accomplishes.
3. **The Praised Disaster:** an ironic account exposes the difference between surviving quickly and carrying an avoidable cost into the next stage.
4. **The Announced Finale:** known attack schedules leave planning and execution unresolved, allowing a direct test of surprise versus mastery.

All retain the user's choice of one hero from three, three boss stages, mid-run items, and separate manual/autoplay demands. Six ordered rules are a candidate autoplay authoring constraint inherited from the prior design. These are paper constructions. Familiar components, narrative delivery and claimed functional additions are distinguished in each card. No mechanic is certified historically original, compatible with every prior contract or capable of improving retention.

The rhetorical question becomes precise: does irony, contradiction or indirection produce a useful change beyond the same literal information? Compare ironic and literal versions of the same rules. Keep a further comparison in which the underlying relation is removed. Otherwise an apparent rhetorical benefit may simply be an information advantage.

## 8. Prepared strategies and proposed study

[LL01–LL12](/home/vlad_/projects/CyberAlchemy-lab/docs/proposals/hammer-bias-strategies/novelty/literature-longevity/2026-10-11/STRATEGIES.md) prepare twelve strategies for generation and investigation. They specify inputs, operations, comparisons, outcomes and defeating observations. They are additions to the repository-local research catalog, not replacements for RT, PR, MG or AC cards and not global skills.

Three compositions provide concrete starting routes:

- **LL02 → LL04 → LL05:** establish the reader's prior knowledge, inspect what a second encounter changes, then build a perspective-based reinterpretation.
- **LL06 → LL09 → LL08:** generate a rhetorical contradiction, attack the build's dependency while retaining useful investments, then distinguish discovery from mastery and surface content.
- **LL10 → LL11 → LL12:** examine selection and exposure, separate private appreciation from transmission, and classify completion versus abandonment before judging endurance.

The [study preparation](/home/vlad_/projects/CyberAlchemy-lab/docs/proposals/hammer-bias-strategies/novelty/literature-longevity/2026-10-11/STUDY-PREPARATION.md) outlines a literary rereading comparison and a longitudinal game comparison. It defines candidate outcomes and missing prerequisites. No sample size, instrument, playable condition, frozen protocol or causal result has been produced. Preparing a card is different from running an experiment.

## 9. What is new in this extension

**New to this research package:** a distinction between individual and cultural persistence; seventeen registered primary sources consisting of twelve research papers, two critical/theoretical essays and three literary works; twelve LL strategies; four worked mechanic constructions; and study preparations that distinguish attraction, discovery, mastery, completion and return. Source counts describe this register and are not a deduplicated increment to every earlier bibliography. The three illustrative classics and the French canonicity study do not represent world literature as a whole.

**Supported research findings:** particular repeated-reading and spoiler experiments, short-term interest findings, novelty/motivation associations, observational retention predictors, and evidence on cultural selection/social influence. Access depth is itemized in [SOURCES.json](/home/vlad_/projects/CyberAlchemy-lab/docs/proposals/hammer-bias-strategies/novelty/literature-longevity/2026-10-11/SOURCES.json); several conclusions are limited to primary abstracts or targeted passages.

**Unestablished:** a universal ingredient list, a causal link from irony to longevity, an expected player lifetime, a new psychological theory, improvement from these strategies, or worldwide originality of the constructions. The existing closed studies and their invocation counts remain separate and preserved.

The next scientific question is specific: **When first-encounter surprise is exhausted, do new interpretations or useful capabilities predict voluntary return beyond the same content, rewards and available time?** The next design question is equally concrete: **Which familiar pieces can be related differently so that a player's growing understanding creates another worthwhile decision?**
