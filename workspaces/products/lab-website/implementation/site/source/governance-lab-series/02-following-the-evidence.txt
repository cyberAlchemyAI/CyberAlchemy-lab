Following the evidence

A customer reports missing rows in a data export. An AI agent finds a log saying the job finished and a screenshot showing fewer rows than expected. The log supports a claim about reported completion. The screenshot supports a claim about what was displayed under particular conditions. CyberAlchemy Lab keeps those claims distinguishable by saving the records, their origins and the conditions under which they support a conclusion.

Provenance is that traceable account of origins and production. For the export, it can identify the customer request, input data, software version, execution conditions, saved output and observation time. It also identifies who recorded or transformed the information. A later reader should be able to tell an original observation from a summary, an adaptation or a generated view.

W3C PROV describes entities, activities and agents: the information or things involved, the processes that produced them and the participants responsible. It provides a vocabulary for tracing derivation and responsibility. The Lab separately records whether a particular source or revision has been accepted to govern a purpose. Knowing where something came from supports assessment; it does not itself supply that acceptance. [W3C PROV-DM](https://www.w3.org/TR/prov-dm/).

The Lab uses separate record kinds for stages of evidence work. A Source identifies a particular origin and how its contents can be identified. An Observation records what was seen or measured in research or an experiment, including conditions and uncertainty. An Operational Observation records information from ordinary project work outside those runs. A Measurement Method specifies how to measure, including units, tools, sampling and uncertainty. A Measurement Result records one execution. An Evidence Set gathers material for a question, including contrary evidence. A Finding states a conclusion whose wording stays within that support.

“The output contains 9,800 rows” is a measurement result when the output and counting method are identifiable. “Two hundred required rows are missing” also needs an inclusion rule and a comparison of expected and actual rows. “The new pagination code caused the omission” needs additional evidence about cause. Pagination means retrieving data in successive batches. The system retains the intermediate records so a confident conclusion cannot replace the comparisons needed to justify it.

A record supports a particular claim under particular conditions. A successful export for one input may establish behavior for that input while leaving larger or changing datasets unexamined. Repeating the same demonstration can support repeatability without expanding those conditions. Evidence must identify the thing and version examined, the scope of the claim and when the evidence is still applicable.

A digest is a digital fingerprint of file contents. It identifies the exact bytes used as evidence. A digital signature can link an approval to a proposal and a signing key under a stated policy. These mechanisms help detect substitution. They do not establish that an observation is true, a test is sufficient or the signer has every claimed permission. Those questions need their own evidence and authority records.

This distinction matters when moving work from CyberAlchemy v2, the earlier source repository, into the Lab. A definition can be copied faithfully while still awaiting acceptance in the Lab. An imported graph can contain many records while omitting current project objects. A source ledger—an ordered collection maintained under stated rules—records what transferred and what remains unresolved, such as renamed identities, missing links and older versions. The Lab calls those meaningful leftovers residue.

Confidence also needs a clear object. Evidence confidence concerns support for a claim. Commitment confidence concerns an owner’s intention to proceed. A team can commit to redesigning the export while evidence about its reliability remains preliminary. The model records these independently. Calling the redesign accepted should not erase uncertainties that future experiments must examine.

A provenance view selects the sources and relationships relevant to an investigation. The agent can trace the export to the software, inputs and measurements, inspect the decisions selecting those versions and find related conclusions. The view must say what it includes, what it omits and whether its sources are still current. Missing or unresolved references remain visible. A drawn path is not confirmation unless the underlying records support it.

A useful conclusion therefore states the comparison performed, the output observed, the software revision used and the remaining uncertainty about cause. It can also identify who is responsible for deciding the next step. Provenance makes the reasoning inspectable, while evidence explains what that reasoning currently supports.
